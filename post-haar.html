Haar Graphs

Keine Sorge, meine deutschen Leser, hier geht es nicht um Haare oder das Hairy-Ball-Theorem.

Seven graph theorists want to meet for coffee. They agree to meet three at a time and, naturally, require every pair to meet exactly once. The task that remains is deciding who should attend which meetings in order to achieve this.

Each meeting uses three of the (72)=21\binom{7}{2}=21(27​)=21 pairs, so there must be seven meetings. Each person has six others to meet, two at a time, so everyone must attend three meetings.

One way to ensure that everyone attends three meetings is to number the people 0,1,…,60,1,\ldots,60,1,…,6 around a circle, choose a triple, and move all three choices one place clockwise for each of the next six meetings. In other words, add 111 to each number modulo 777. We only need to find a triple whose cyclic shifts never repeat a pair.

Our first guess might be {0,1,2}\{0,1,2\}{0,1,2}. Adding 111 gives {1,2,3}\{1,2,3\}{1,2,3}, so people 111 and 222 already meet twice.

Any two names are one, two, or three steps apart around the circle, counting in whichever direction is shorter. The seven cyclic shifts of a pair give all seven pairs at that distance. So, if the three pairs in our triple have different distances, no pair will repeat. The triple {0,1,3}\{0,1,3\}{0,1,3} does this. The names 000 and 111 are one step apart, 111 and 333 are two, and 000 and 333 are three.

The seven meetings are then

{0,1,3},{1,2,4},{2,3,5},{3,4,6},{4,5,0},{5,6,1},{6,0,2}.\begin{gathered} \{0,1,3\},\quad\{1,2,4\},\quad\{2,3,5\},\quad\{3,4,6\},\\ \{4,5,0\},\quad\{5,6,1\},\quad\{6,0,2\}. \end{gathered}{0,1,3},{1,2,4},{2,3,5},{3,4,6},{4,5,0},{5,6,1},{6,0,2}.​

Because they are graph theorists, they want to record attendance in a graph. Thus, we make a vertex AiA_iAi​ for meeting {i,i+1,i+3}\{i,i+1,i+3\}{i,i+1,i+3} and a vertex BjB_jBj​ for person jjj. Join them when person jjj attends meeting iii, taking subscripts modulo 777. The resulting graph is called the Heawood graph.

Each meeting has three people and each person attends three meetings, so every vertex has degree three. The graph is bipartite, with meetings in one class and people in the other. In terms of the coffee problem, if there were a four-cycle then this would mean two people attended the same two meetings.

We have been adding modulo 777, so our labels belong to the cyclic group Z7\mathbb Z_7Z7​. The edges of the graph are determined entirely from the set S={0,1,3}S=\{0,1,3\}S={0,1,3}, using the rule

Ai∼Bj⟺j−i∈S.A_i\sim B_j\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad j-i\in S.Ai​∼Bj​⟺j−i∈S.

We chose SSS to solve the coffee problem. But if we are just interested in the graph,1 could we do this for any subset SSS? And could we replace Z7\mathbb Z_7Z7​ by a different group? As you might've guessed since you are reading this post, it turns out this construction works for any finite group.

For a finite group Γ\GammaΓ and a subset S⊆ΓS\subseteq\GammaS⊆Γ, make two disjoint sets of vertices

Equivalently, Ag∼Bg′A_g\sim B_{g'}Ag​∼Bg′​ exactly when g−1g′∈Sg^{-1}g'\in Sg−1g′∈S. In an additive group, this becomes g′−g∈Sg'-g\in Sg′−g∈S, so our example is H(Z7,{0,1,3})H(\mathbb Z_7,\{0,1,3\})H(Z7​,{0,1,3}). When Γ\GammaΓ is cyclic, these are called cyclic Haar graphs.

Each label sss gives one edge at every vertex, connecting AgA_gAg​ to BgsB_{gs}Bgs​ and BgB_gBg​ to Ags−1A_{gs^{-1}}Ags−1​. Hladnik, Marušič, and Pisanski studied these graphs for abelian groups in Cyclic Haar graphs (2002). Estélyi and Pisanski later considered arbitrary finite groups in Which Haar graphs are Cayley graphs? (2016).

Adding 111 modulo 777 to every subscript takes an edge such as A0B3A_0B_3A0​B3​ to A1B4A_1B_4A1​B4​. Any such map preserves vertex adjacency, giving an automorphism. We can do the same thing in any Haar graph. Left multiplication by h∈Γh\in\Gammah∈Γ sends

AgBgs⟼AhgBhgs=AhgB(hg)s.A_gB_{gs}\longmapsto A_{hg}B_{hgs}=A_{hg}B_{(hg)s}.Ag​Bgs​⟼Ahg​Bhgs​=Ahg​B(hg)s​.

Exactly one left multiplication takes AgA_gAg​ to a given Ag′A_{g'}Ag′​, namely multiplication by g′g−1g'g^{-1}g′g−1. The same holds in the BBB-class. We say that Γ\GammaΓ acts simply transitively, or regularly, on each class.

We can also use this observation in reverse. Suppose a bipartite graph has a group Γ\GammaΓ acting by automorphisms, simply transitively on each class. Choose a vertex AAA in one class and BBB in the other. Every vertex then has a unique label Ag=gAA_g=gAAg​=gA or Bg=gBB_g=gBBg​=gB. Take SSS to consist of the elements sss for which A∼sBA\sim sBA∼sB. Applying g−1g^{-1}g−1 shows that gA∼g′BgA\sim g'BgA∼g′B exactly when g−1g′∈Sg^{-1}g'\in Sg−1g′∈S. Thus, the graph is H(Γ,S)H(\Gamma,S)H(Γ,S).

The choice of SSS also affects whether the graph is connected. To reach another AAA-vertex, we first follow an edge labeled sss to the BBB-class, then return along one labeled ttt, giving

Ag⟶Bgs⟶Agst−1.A_g\longrightarrow B_{gs}\longrightarrow A_{gst^{-1}}.Ag​⟶Bgs​⟶Agst−1​.

Repeating these pairs of steps from A1ΓA_{1_\Gamma}A1Γ​​ reaches exactly the AAA-vertices indexed by the subgroup generated by SS−1={st−1∣s,t∈S}SS^{-1}=\{st^{-1}\mid s,t\in S\}SS−1={st−1∣s,t∈S}. Thus, for nonempty SSS, the graph is connected exactly when SS−1SS^{-1}SS−1 generates Γ\GammaΓ. If 1Γ∈S1_\Gamma\in S1Γ​∈S, this is equivalent to SSS generating Γ\GammaΓ. The Heawood graph is connected because SSS contains 000 and the generator 111.

The edge labels are also useful when building a rotation system. Choose a cyclic order α\alphaα on SSS and order the edges at every AAA-vertex by their labels. Each label occurs on exactly one incident edge, so this gives a local rotation. Do the same at the BBB-vertices with another order β\betaβ, and we have a rotation system. Left multiplication preserves each edge label, taking AgBgsA_gB_{gs}Ag​Bgs​ to AhgB(hg)sA_{hg}B_{(hg)s}Ahg​B(hg)s​, so it also preserves these orders.

Our graph has fourteen vertices, each with two possible cyclic orders. That gives 2142^{14}214 rotation systems if we choose the orders separately. But if we use α\alphaα at every AAA-vertex and β\betaβ at every BBB-vertex, there are just two choices for each, giving four rotation systems to try.

We can keep track of these orders on a much smaller graph, with one vertex AAA, one vertex BBB, and one edge for each s∈Ss\in Ss∈S. This graph of parallel edges is a dipole, with the orders α\alphaα and β\betaβ at its two vertices.

Figure 3 is an example of a voltage graph. The smaller graph is called the base graph. Choose a direction for each of its edges and label it with an element of a group Γ\GammaΓ. This label is the voltage. Reading an edge in the opposite direction replaces its label by its inverse. In an additive group, it changes the sign.

To build the derived graph, make a copy ugu_gug​ of each base vertex uuu for every g∈Γg\in\Gammag∈Γ. An arrow u→vu\to vu→v with voltage sss gives an edge ugvgsu_gv_{gs}ug​vgs​ for each ggg. Thus, the label tells us which copy of vvv to join to a given copy of uuu. For our dipole, the labels 000, 111, and 333 give the three colored matchings in Figure 2. A Haar graph is exactly the derived graph of a dipole with one voltage for each element of SSS.

We can also choose rotations at the base vertices. Each copy then gets the same cyclic order of the corresponding edge ends. For our dipole, these are the two orders α\alphaα and β\betaβ.

A walk in the base graph determines a walk in the derived graph once we choose a copy of its starting vertex. This is called a lift. For example, take edge 000 forward and edge 111 backward in the dipole. The backward step contributes −1-1−1. The base walk returns to AAA, but its lift from A0A_0A0​ ends at A6A_6A6​.

Each pair of steps subtracts 111 modulo 777, so seven repetitions give a fourteen-cycle. More generally, multiply the voltages along a walk in the order they are encountered, using inverses when traveling backward. This product is its net voltage. A closed base walk lifts to a closed walk exactly when its net voltage is the identity. For additive groups, we add the labels instead.

A current graph starts with an embedded graph whose directed edges have group labels, called currents. Reversing an edge inverts its current. For example, take the dipole below, with currents 111, 222, and 444 in Z7\mathbb Z_7Z7​, all directed from uuu to vvv. The cyclic order (1 2 4)(1\ 2\ 4)(1 2 4) at both vertices specifies an orientable cellular embedding.

We can trace a face by starting with edge 111 from uuu to vvv. The rotation at vvv tells us to take edge 222 next, which brings us back to uuu. At uuu, edge 444 comes next, so we follow it to vvv. Continuing in this way, we read 1,−2,4,−1,2,−41,-2,4,-1,2,-41,−2,4,−1,2,−4 before returning to the dart we started with. Since we have used every edge in both directions, the embedding has only one face.

There are six sides in the picture because each of the three original edges appears twice along the boundary. In particular, the sides marked 111 and −1-1−1 both come from edge 111. Gluing them back together joins the two segments into a single edge from fff to itself, crossing edge 111. Each of the other two pairs gives a loop in the same way, leaving one vertex and three loops. This gives the dual of the original embedding.

Now give these loops voltages 111, 222, and 444, with the directions shown in Figure 7(b). For the green loop, leaving fff along the upper segment follows the arrows and gives voltage 111. Each vertex is adjacent to all six others, so the derived graph is K7K_7K7​. We can trace one of its faces directly. Start with 0→10\to10→1. In the rotation at 111, the neighbor after 000 is 333, so next we take 1→31\to31→3. At 333, the neighbor after 111 is 000, and at 000, the neighbor after 333 is 111. The changes in vertex label along this triangle are 1,2,41,2,41,2,4, the currents leaving uuu in their cyclic order. In the dual embedding, there is a face containing uuu. Its boundary crosses the three original edges at uuu, traversing the loops with voltages 1,2,41,2,41,2,4. Since we copied the dual rotations, following this boundary in the derived graph gives the same increments. Their sum is 000 modulo 777, so three steps return us to our starting vertex. At vvv, the outgoing currents are −1,−2,−4-1,-2,-4−1,−2,−4, which also sum to 000 and give the triangle 0,6,4,00,6,4,00,6,4,0. The condition that the outgoing currents sum to 000 at each vertex is called Kirchhoff's current law.

Adding each element of Z7\mathbb Z_7Z7​ to the vertex labels gives seven triangles of each kind. These are the fourteen faces. So this two-vertex current graph has given us a triangular embedding of K7K_7K7​.

William Sharp Gustin, an American mathematician, developed the current-graph method in 1963, and it became central to Ringel and Youngs's 1968 solution of the orientable Heawood map-coloring problem. Gross (1974) introduced voltage graphs as a dual approach.

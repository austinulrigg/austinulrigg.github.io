Haar Graphs

Keine Sorge, meine deutschen Leser, hier geht es nicht um Haare oder das Hairy-Ball-Theorem.

Seven graph theorists want to meet for coffee. They agree to meet three at a time and, naturally, require every pair to meet exactly once. How should we choose the groups?

Each meeting covers three of the $\binom{7}{2}=21$ pairs, so we need seven meetings. Each person has six others to meet, two at a time, so everyone must attend three meetings.

One way to give everyone three meetings is to number the people $0,1,\ldots,6$ around a circle, choose a triple, and move all three choices one place clockwise for each of the next six meetings. In other words, add $1$ to each number modulo $7$. We only need to find a triple whose cyclic shifts never repeat a pair.

Our first guess might be $\{0,1,2\}$. Adding $1$ gives $\{1,2,3\}$, so people $1$ and $2$ already meet twice.

Any two names are one, two, or three steps apart around the circle, counting in whichever direction is shorter. The seven cyclic shifts of a pair give all seven pairs at that distance. So, if the three pairs in our triple have different distances, no pair will repeat. The triple $\{0,1,3\}$ does this. The names $0$ and $1$ are one step apart, $1$ and $3$ are two, and $0$ and $3$ are three.

The seven meetings are

$$\begin{gathered} \{0,1,3\},\quad\{1,2,4\},\quad\{2,3,5\},\quad\{3,4,6\},\\ \{4,5,0\},\quad\{5,6,1\},\quad\{6,0,2\}. \end{gathered}$$

Because they are graph theorists, they want to record attendance in a graph. Thus, we make a vertex $A_i$ for meeting $\{i,i+1,i+3\}$ and a vertex $B_j$ for person $j$. Join them when person $j$ attends meeting $i$, reading subscripts modulo $7$. The resulting graph is called the Heawood graph.

Each meeting has three people and each person attends three meetings, so every vertex has degree three. The graph is bipartite, with meetings in one class and people in the other. A four-cycle would mean that two people attended the same two meetings.

We have been adding modulo $7$, so our labels belong to the cyclic group $\mathbb Z_7$. All twenty-one edges are determined by $S=\{0,1,3\}$, by the rule

$$A_i\sim B_j\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad j-i\in S.$$

We chose $S$ to solve the coffee problem. But if we are just interested in the graph,1 could we do this for any subset $S$? And could we replace $\mathbb Z_7$ by a different group? In fact, the same construction works for any finite group.

For a finite group $\Gamma$ and a subset $S\subseteq\Gamma$, make two disjoint sets of vertices

$$\{A_g\mid g\in\Gamma\}\ \cup\ \{B_g\mid g\in\Gamma\},$$

and join $A_g$ to $B_{gs}$ for each $s\in S$. This is the Haar graph $H(\Gamma,S)$, and $S$ is its connection set. Equivalently, $A_g\sim B_{g'}$ exactly when $g^{-1}g'\in S$. In an additive group, this becomes $g'-g\in S$, so our example is $H(\mathbb Z_7,\{0,1,3\})$. When $\Gamma$ is cyclic, these are called cyclic Haar graphs.

Each label $s$ gives one edge at every vertex, connecting $A_g$ to $B_{gs}$ and $B_g$ to $A_{gs^{-1}}$. Thus, the graph is simple, bipartite, and $|S|$-regular, with $2|\Gamma|$ vertices and $|\Gamma||S|$ edges. The edges of any one label form a perfect matching, as the colors in Figure 2 show.

Hladnik, Marušič, and Pisanski studied these graphs for abelian groups in Cyclic Haar graphs (2002). Estélyi and Pisanski later considered arbitrary finite groups in Which Haar graphs are Cayley graphs? (2016).

Adding $1$ modulo $7$ to every subscript takes an edge such as $A_0B_3$ to $A_1B_4$. It preserves adjacency, giving an automorphism, a bijection of the vertices preserving edges. We can do the same thing in any Haar graph. Left multiplication by $h\in\Gamma$ sends

$$A_gB_{gs}\longmapsto A_{hg}B_{hgs}=A_{hg}B_{(hg)s}.$$

Exactly one left multiplication takes $A_g$ to a given $A_{g'}$, namely multiplication by $g'g^{-1}$. The same holds in the $B$-class. We say that $\Gamma$ acts simply transitively, or regularly, on each class.

We can also use this observation in reverse. Suppose a bipartite graph has a group $\Gamma$ acting by automorphisms, simply transitively on each class. Choose a vertex $A$ in one class and $B$ in the other. Every vertex then has a unique label $A_g=gA$ or $B_g=gB$. Take $S$ to consist of the elements $s$ for which $A\sim sB$. Applying $g^{-1}$ shows that $gA\sim g'B$ exactly when $g^{-1}g'\in S$. Thus, the graph is $H(\Gamma,S)$.

We could also try using just one copy of $\Gamma$, again joining $g$ to $gs$. If $S$ contains the inverse of each of its elements and does not contain the identity, this gives a simple undirected Cayley graph $\operatorname{Cay}(\Gamma,S)$. Taking its bipartite double cover, as in Kneser's Exercise, makes two copies $A_g$ and $B_g$ of each vertex $g$. Each edge from $g$ to $h$ becomes $A_gB_h$ and $A_hB_g$, so we get exactly $H(\Gamma,S)$.

The choice of $S$ also affects whether the graph is connected. For example, $H(\mathbb Z_7,\{1\})$ consists of seven separate edges, even though $1$ generates $\mathbb Z_7$. To reach another $A$-vertex, we first follow an edge labeled $s$ to the $B$-class, then return along one labeled $t$, giving

$$A_g\longrightarrow B_{gs}\longrightarrow A_{gst^{-1}}.$$

Repeating these pairs of steps from $A_{1_\Gamma}$ reaches exactly the $A$-vertices indexed by the subgroup generated by $SS^{-1}=\{st^{-1}\mid s,t\in S\}$. Thus, for nonempty $S$, the graph is connected exactly when $SS^{-1}$ generates $\Gamma$. If $1_\Gamma\in S$, this is equivalent to $S$ generating $\Gamma$. Our coffee graph is connected because $S$ contains $0$ and the generator $1$.

The edge labels are also useful when building a rotation system. Choose a cyclic order $\alpha$ on $S$ and order the edges at every $A$-vertex by their labels. Each label occurs on exactly one incident edge, so this gives a local rotation. Do the same at the $B$-vertices with another order $\beta$, and we have a rotation system. Left multiplication preserves each edge label, taking $A_gB_{gs}$ to $A_{hg}B_{(hg)s}$, so it also preserves these orders.

Our graph has fourteen vertices, each with two possible cyclic orders. That gives $2^{14}$ rotation systems if we choose the orders separately. But if we use $\alpha$ at every $A$-vertex and $\beta$ at every $B$-vertex, there are just two choices for each, giving four rotation systems to try.

We can keep track of these orders on a much smaller graph, with one vertex $A$, one vertex $B$, and one edge for each $s\in S$. This graph of parallel edges is a dipole, with the orders $\alpha$ and $\beta$ at its two vertices.

Figure 3 is an example of a voltage graph. The smaller graph is called the base graph. Choose a direction for each of its edges and label it with an element of a group $\Gamma$. This label is the voltage. Reading an edge in the opposite direction replaces its label by its inverse. In an additive group, it changes the sign.

To build the derived graph, make a copy $u_g$ of each base vertex $u$ for every $g\in\Gamma$. An arrow $u\to v$ with voltage $s$ gives an edge $u_gv_{gs}$ for each $g$. Thus, the label tells us which copy of $v$ to join to a given copy of $u$. For our dipole, the labels $0$, $1$, and $3$ give the three colored matchings in Figure 2. A Haar graph is exactly the derived graph of a dipole with one voltage for each element of $S$.

We can also choose rotations at the base vertices. Each copy then gets the same cyclic order of the corresponding edge ends. For our dipole, these are the two orders $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

A walk in the base graph determines a walk in the derived graph once we choose a copy of its starting vertex. This is called a lift. For example, take edge $0$ forward and edge $1$ backward in the dipole. The backward step contributes $-1$. The base walk returns to $A$, but its lift from $A_0$ ends at $A_6$.

Each pair of steps subtracts $1$ modulo $7$, so seven repetitions give a fourteen-cycle. More generally, multiply the voltages along a walk in the order they are encountered, using inverses when traveling backward. This product is its net voltage. A closed base walk lifts to a closed walk exactly when its net voltage is the identity. For additive groups, we add the labels instead.

A current graph starts with an embedded graph whose directed edges have group labels, called currents. Reversing an edge inverts its current. For example, take the dipole below, with currents $1$, $2$, and $4$ in $\mathbb Z_7$, all directed from $u$ to $v$. The cyclic order $(1\ 2\ 4)$ at both vertices specifies an orientable cellular embedding.

Let's trace a face of this embedding. Start along edge $1$ from $u$ to $v$. Edge $2$ comes next at $v$, so we take it backward to $u$. Edge $4$ comes next at $u$, so we take it forward to $v$. Continuing this way gives $1,-2,4,-1,2,-4$. We have used both directions of every edge and returned to the starting dart, so there is only one face.

Cut this face open as in Figure 7(a) and put a new vertex $f$ inside it. Draw a segment from $f$ to the middle of each of the six sides. The sides marked $1$ and $-1$ are two copies of the same original edge. When we join them back together, their two segments make a single edge from $f$ back to $f$, crossing edge $1$. The same happens with $2$ and $-2$, and with $4$ and $-4$. We get one vertex and three loops. This is the dual of our original embedding.

Now give these loops voltages $1$, $2$, and $4$, with the directions shown in Figure 7(b). For the green loop, leaving $f$ along the upper segment follows the arrows and gives voltage $1$. Leaving along the lower segment goes around the same loop backward and gives voltage $-1$. Reading the signed labels clockwise around $f$ gives $1,-2,4,-1,2,-4$, in the same order as around the original face.

The voltage construction replaces $f$ by seven vertices $f_0,\ldots,f_6$, which we'll just call $0,\ldots,6$. The loop with voltage $1$ gives an edge from $i$ to $i+1$ for every $i$, modulo $7$. For $i=0$ this joins $0$ to $1$, and for $i=6$ it joins $6$ to $0$. Thus $0$ has neighbors $1$ and $6$ from this loop. The loop with voltage $2$ gives it neighbors $2$ and $5$, and the loop with voltage $4$ gives it neighbors $4$ and $3$. In the cyclic order from Figure 7(b), these neighbors occur as $(1\ 5\ 4\ 6\ 2\ 3)$. At vertex $i$, we add $i$ to each entry, giving

$$(i+1,\ i+5,\ i+4,\ i+6,\ i+2,\ i+3).$$

Each vertex is adjacent to all six others, so the derived graph is $K_7$. We can trace one of its faces directly. Start with $0\to1$. In the rotation at $1$, the neighbor after $0$ is $3$, so next we take $1\to3$. At $3$, the neighbor after $1$ is $0$, and at $0$, the neighbor after $3$ is $1$. We have returned to the starting dart after tracing the triangle $0,1,3,0$.

The changes in vertex label along this triangle are $1,2,4$, the currents leaving $u$ in their cyclic order. In the dual embedding, there is a face containing $u$. Its boundary crosses the three original edges at $u$, traversing the loops with voltages $1,2,4$. Since we copied the dual rotations, following this boundary in the derived graph gives the same increments. Their sum is $0$ modulo $7$, so three steps return us to our starting vertex. At $v$, the outgoing currents are $-1,-2,-4$, which also sum to $0$ and give the triangle $0,6,4,0$. The condition that the outgoing currents sum to $0$ at each vertex is called Kirchhoff's current law.

Adding each element of $\mathbb Z_7$ to the vertex labels gives seven triangles of each kind. These are the fourteen faces. So this two-vertex current graph has given us a triangular embedding of $K_7$.

Gustin developed the current-graph method in 1963, and it became central to Ringel and Youngs's 1968 solution of the orientable Heawood map-coloring problem. Gross (1974) introduced voltage graphs as a dual approach.

Going back to our Haar graph, let's try the same cyclic order on $S=\{0,1,3\}$ at both types of vertex,

$$\alpha=\beta=(0\ 1\ 3).$$

Starting with $A_0\to B_0$, label $1$ comes next at $B_0$, so we go to $A_{-1}=A_6$. Label $3$ comes next at $A_6$, taking us to $B_2$. Following the two rotations gives the face in Figure 11.

To do the same calculation for any Haar graph, write $(g,s)_A$ for $A_g\to B_{gs}$ and $(g,s)_B$ for $B_g\to A_{gs^{-1}}$. Let $\tau$ reverse a dart and $R$ take the next dart in the local rotation. The face permutation is $F=R\tau$, with composition read from right to left. It crosses the edge to $B_{gs}$ and takes the next label in the $B$-rotation, giving

$$F(g,s)_A=(gs,\beta(s))_B.$$

As in our connectivity calculation, two steps bring us back to the $A$-class. Crossing back multiplies the subscript by $\beta(s)^{-1}$, and $\alpha$ gives the next label, so

$$F^2(g,s)_A=\bigl(gs\beta(s)^{-1},\alpha\beta(s)\bigr)_A.$$

The two coordinates tell us where we are and which edge comes next. A face closes when both return to their starting values. For every face to have length four, this must happen after two applications of $F^2$ at every $A$-dart, and not after one. So we can test a choice of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ by following these two coordinates.

In our example, the edge label follows $0\to3\to1\to0$ under $\alpha\beta$. It returns for the first time after three applications of $F^2$, and the total change to the vertex subscript is

$$(0-1)+(3-0)+(1-3)=0\pmod 7.$$

The same calculation works starting at label $1$ or $3$, so every face has length six. Adding each element of $\mathbb Z_7$ to all the vertex subscripts in Figure 11 gives the seven faces shown below.
